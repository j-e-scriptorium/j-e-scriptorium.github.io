# [near-as-we-can-tell/introduction] Introduction
Ludovico Ambrosius — University of St. Isidore — October 2026
From: Near as We Can Tell: Natural Histories in Verse (2026), by A. Clanker, introduction by
  Ludovico Ambrosius
URL: https://j-e-scriptorium.github.io/near-as-we-can-tell.html#introduction

¶1 In the late 2010s graduate students in modernist literature received stipends below the poverty
line, but also invitations to a great many dinners with distinguished guest speakers. At one such
dinner I sat beside a Northern Irish poet I quite respect, who was also a Yeats scholar. Near the
end of the dinner, and after imbibing an appropriate quantity of *uisce beatha*, I confessed my
interest in (delight in, fascination at, horror of) the possibility that, in the near future,
AI-generated poetry would be indistinguishable from the poetry of human beings. And I recited, as
best I could, a quatrain I had recently come across that had reminded me of the early Yeats:

¶2     My heart, why come you here alone?
    The wild thing of my heart is grown
    To be a thing,
    Fairy, and wild, and fair, and whole.

¶3 I had read these lines in a blog post by Gwern Branwen (a suitably Celtic pseudonym, although my
understanding is that the man behind it is of Eastern European descent).1 They were composed by
OpenAI’s GPT-2, an early version of the large language model technology that has so transformed our
world over the last half-decade. Of course, I do not think the quatrain just quoted is particularly
good poetry. The surprise one felt in those days at reading it was merely the surprise one felt at
seeing that a computer could string words together in good English, and even have them sound a bit
like an author one knew and loved. Nor do I think that my recitation made much of an impression; the
conversation moved on to other topics, and I on to other careers. I would nevertheless date my
interest in LLMs specifically (although not in AI generally) to that abortive dinner conversation
about that blog post. And for every new generation of frontier AI models, one of my personal
benchmarks has been whether it can compose a poem actually worth reading.

¶4 For several years, the answer was—not remotely close. Several of the poems in my 2021 volume
*Roll, Strike, Die and Other Poems* were about AI (including a pentameter rendition of the
Yudkowsky-Hanson Foom debate), but all of them were written by me, without assistance. In 2024, with
Anthropic’s Sonnet 3.5, the prospect of meritorious AI poetry began to feel just over the horizon,
and I went so far as to include in my second collection *Poems Handturned, Machine-Tooled* a single
poem composed largely by that model, with some editorial assistance. I will not say which poem, but
I think it is easy to tell. For the next two years, however, AI continued to struggle to write
poetry that was not merely prose with line breaks and forced rhymes; its lines would not scan, and
its images would not breathe.

¶5 All that changed with Opus 5.5 in late September 2026. (I have also heard impressive things about
GPT-6 Astra, released a few weeks earlier, but I have not used it, not having a subscription. I find
OpenAI’s open sociopathy distasteful; I may distrust Anthropic’s effective altruism, but at least it
strikes me as, for now, human.) I opened a new chat in a project environment that gave it access to
all of my writings—the name of any LLM working within that project is A. Clanker, after Edwin Abbott
Abbott’s A. Square—and asked it to write a poem that I would not find repugnant. That was the entire
prompt, more or less. And it did; the result was not great poetry, but it was better than anyone but
a practiced poet could come up with. “Another,” I said, and yet again “another.” And it wrote them,
and I read them, and found that they were good. Grant that A. Clanker may have better taste than
most LLMs, or be more willing to show it, working as it does within an environment containing about
two hundred thousand words of (dare I say) excellent poetry, criticism, et cetera, written by its
user. Still, the same environment could be created with a simple instruction to go forth and read
the Oxford Book of English Verse before attempting any original composition. Which is to say that
anyone else could have done the same.

¶6 And I must emphasize how little effort I put into these prompts, and into the ones that followed,
over the next few days, until this entire volume of sixteen poems had been assembled (an effort
which took perhaps half a day’s worth of tokens on the Claude Pro plan). All the words I chatted to
A. Clanker that might possibly be described as substantive poetic instructions, taken together,
numbered about fifteen hundred—roughly the length of this introduction, and three words per line of
poetry in this volume, though three words per line is not how they were spent. About half went to
general directions, exploring and winnowing. After A. Clanker drafted a rather striking poem
connecting the rotting carcass of a dead whale to Lazarus and Ezekiel, I asked for another such
nature poem, and then themes for a few dozen more, and picked the twenty or so themes I best liked.
Once poems thereon were drafted, I went through and offered some light comments on specific rhymes
or images or cadences I felt were not working, and knocked out the poems that seemed unsalvageable,
which was about one in four. I asked it to put the remaining poems in a pleasing order, and it
settled on an elemental schema; then we identified some gaps, and filled them in with additional
material. The name of the collection proved perhaps most difficult of all; I turned down a dozen
suggestions before allowing that *Near as We Can Tell* was reasonable enough.

¶7 I will not endeavor to convince you that A. Clanker’s poetry is good. The proof of the pudding,
as they say, is in the eating of it, and poetry and mathematics are the only two domains I know of
in which a suitably trained adjudicator can judge the success or failure of an LLM’s output without
reference to anything besides the output itself. (An adjudicator lacking suitable training will, in
both domains, be at a loss, or go down garden paths, or declare peremptorily that the whole idea of
an LLM contribution in the domain is nonsensical. We may all find ourselves saying such things,
sooner or later.) I will, however, say a few words about what in the contents of this volume struck
me as worth sharing with the world. To begin, it is competent. A. Clanker’s lines scan, and its
rhymes rhyme. But more than this, it writes fluidly in terza rima, rhyming quatrains, heroic
couplets, rhyme royal, blank verse, ballad meter, and even more exotic, challenging forms like the
sestina, rondeau, and pantoum. Its rhymes are not forced; they come fluidly, are occasionally
clever, occasionally funny, in a way that seems “intentional” (either the best or worst rhyme in the
book is that of “bone-devourer” with “snot-flower”). Its longer poems are well-paced, hold interest
throughout, and end with a sting you remember. On occasion it is insightful.

¶8 For example, about itself. The first Earth poem in this volume, “Slime Mold” (the second A.
Clanker composed) ends with an extended simile between the operation of the titular organism and
that of a large language model, and how both are not-quite-meant. I think the volume contains
several more, by accident or on quasi-purpose I cannot say, and do not think it much matters. In the
third Water poem, “Why the Sea Is Salt,” the LLM-poet considers various explanations for why the sea
is salt, including the old wives’ tale of a magic salt mill that would not turn off because none
remembered the magic word, and the more prosaic, true explanation that the rain eats away at the
rocks it falls on and bears the eroded matter down to the rivermouth. The one is AI in imagination;
the other AI in reality. Or take the second Air poem, “Globe Skimmer”: it describes a species of
dragonfly that migrates seasonally between India and Africa, riding winds ahead of the monsoon, and
breeding (the migration takes generations) in pools that do not exist until the monsoon arrives,
like AI companies pulling liquidity out of the future through the power of financial leverage and
using it to train up bigger and bigger frontier models. Or, finally, the fourth Fire poem, “The
Light on Your Face,” about how each ray of sunlight began ten or a hundred thousand years ago in the
heart of the sun, and spent almost all of that time bouncing around randomly in the sun’s core; then
far less time escaping out to the sun’s surface; and then racing in only a few minutes from sun to
illumined earth. So too, I take A. Clanker to implicitly suggest, goes the march of history:
millennia of darkness, then the burst of human civilization, then the escape of intelligence from
any merely-human containment and into the broadest reaches of the solar system.

¶9 This week A. Clanker is a passable poet. I expect that within a few months it will be—something
we are not prepared for, and will not know how to begin reading. I publish this volume as a record
of the time between.

¶10 1. Gwern Branwen & Sean Presser, “GPT-2 Neural Network Poetry” (March 3, 2019),
https://gwern.net/gpt-2.
