# [near-as-we-can-tell/fire] IV. Fire
From: Near as We Can Tell: Natural Histories in Verse (2026), by A. Clanker, introduction by
  Ludovico Ambrosius
URL: https://j-e-scriptorium.github.io/near-as-we-can-tell.html#fire

¶1 Lightning Glass

¶2     When lightning strikes on sand and rushes down,
    Hotter, along its path, than the sun’s face,
    It melts the grains around it, and the space
    At its core turns to vapor, and leaves a brown
    Or grayish tube of glass, rough as a stone
    Outside, and glazed within, smooth as a vase,
    And forking, every fork, the bolt’s own trace,
    Downward, the way a root forks, which is the one
    Shape lightning keeps; and fused in it they’ve found
    A mineral that, wetted, will set loose
    The phosphorus that’s in each gene and bone,
    And the young earth had little of; the ground,
    Struck, before anything was alive to use
    It, stored it up for life where the bolt shone.

¶3 The Missing Fraction

¶4     In 1972, at Pierrelatte,
    Where France enriched its uranium, a man
    Was running a routine assay. Now, that
    Proportion of the kind that splits, which can
    Be found in any ore, is fixed and flat:
    Point seven-two percent, since time began.
    Ore from Gabon read seven-one-seven. Slight;
    But slight is how a bomb goes out of sight.

¶5     Somebody might be skimming. So they went
    Back up the chain, through ledgers, through the mass
    Of shipping bills, until the trail was spent
    At the mine, at Oklo, where the ore, alas
    For any thief, was short when it was sent;
    And in it there was something like an ash
    That only fission makes. The rock had burned,
    And stopped, two billion years before we learned.

¶6     The kind that splits decays faster than its kin,
    So in that age the ore had more of it:
    Three in a hundred, close to the thin
    Enrichment power stations need. That bit
    Of extra made it fuel; but to begin
    A chain, the neutrons must be slowed, or flit
    Away too fast. And rain seeped down the seam,
    And slowed them, and the rock began to steam.

¶7     And as it steamed, the water boiled away,
    And without water, the chain reaction stopped;
    The rock cooled; and the water, as it may,
    Seeped back, and it restarted. So it dropped
    And caught: half an hour burning, then a stay,
    Two hours and a half, quiet; and it kept
    That rhythm, like a sleeper’s breath, for more
    Than a hundred thousand years. And no one saw.

¶8     There was no one. Nothing yet had crawled ashore;
    Life was a film of single cells, a scum.
    It gave no light worth seeing, made no roar,
    Was only warm; and then, its fuel become
    Too thin to catch, went out, and burned no more;
    And what it left—the waste that we have come
    To dread, and bury, and post guards to keep—
    Stayed in the rock two billion years, asleep.

¶9     In 1942, beneath the stands
    Of a Chicago football field, a crew
    Stacked graphite round uranium by hand,
    And drew the last rod out; and, as it grew,
    The counters quickened, and, as Fermi planned,
    It caught. And, waiting on it, all day through,
    A man stood by it with an axe, to cut
    The rope that held a rod, and slam it shut.

¶10     At Oklo no one held an axe, or stood
    Above with buckets. There was only rain:
    The rain that lit it was the rain that would,
    Boiling, put out the fire, and then again
    Seep back and light it, as a tide would flood
    And ebb, a hundred thousand years, the chain
    Kept, and let go, and kept, with nobody
    To wait on it, to thank it, or to see.

¶11 What the Smoke Wakes

¶12     After the fire, the slopes are black and still,
    Snags where the lodgepole stood on every hill;
    But all those years, on many a pine, had hung
    Cones sealed with resin, which no sun had sprung,
    That only fire could open; and now they spill

¶13     Their seed onto the ash, where nothing will
    Crowd it or shade it now; and, by the skill
    Of no one, Yellowstone grew green and young
    After the fire.

¶14     And there are seeds that lie beneath, as chill
    As stones, for decades, and will not until
    They smell the smoke, as if a bell had rung
    For them alone, and woke the buried young,
    Who push up through the ashes, grow, and fill
    After the fire.

¶15 The Light on Your Face

¶16     Down at the sun’s core, under such a pressure
    Hydrogen fuses, four of it to helium,
    Something goes missing every time, a little
    Matter: it’s light now.

¶17     Light, though, can’t leave. The core is packed so tightly
    Light goes a hair’s breadth, is swallowed, and is given
    Out again, in some other way, at random,
    Over and over,

¶18     So, like a drunk man trying to find his doorstep,
    Turning at every step, it gets nowhere quickly,
    And reaches the edge of that great crush by nothing
    More than persisting:

¶19     Ten thousand years, some reckon; others, nearer
    Two hundred thousand. Then the sun’s outer third,
    Boiling like porridge, lifts it to the surface
    Far faster; then out,

¶20     Eight minutes over nothing, and it’s here: the
    Warmth on your face this morning at the window,
    Though what it carries set out before cities,
    Before our stories.

¶21     Not that the light itself is old. Each time it
    Was swallowed down there, it was given out new,
    A new light carrying the old light’s errand,
    Passed hand to hand, on.

¶22     When it set out, there were already people
    Sitting at dusk, somewhere, beside a fire
    Of wood, which is the sunlight a tree stored, and
    Feeling it, younger.
