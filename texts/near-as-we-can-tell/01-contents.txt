¶1 Introduction

¶2 I. Earth

¶3 *Slime Mold*

¶4 *Physarum polycephalum* is a single cell, sometimes as wide as a plate, with millions of nuclei
and no nervous system. It finds the shortest route through a maze between two food sources by
thickening the tubes that carry the most flow and abandoning the rest.

¶5 T. Nakagaki, H. Yamada & Á. Tóth, “Maze-Solving by an Amoeboid Organism,” *Nature* 407 (2000):
470.

¶6 *Map Lichen*

¶7 Crustose lichens of the genus *Rhizocarpon* grow so slowly and steadily that the largest patch on
a rock dates how long the rock has been exposed. Calibrated against gravestones of known date,
lichen sizes on moraines date the retreat of glaciers.

¶8 R. E. Beschel, “Flechten als Altersmaßstab rezenter Moränen,” *Zeitschrift für Gletscherkunde und
Glazialgeologie* 1 (1950): 152–61.

¶9 *The Yellow Underneath*

¶10 The yellows and oranges of autumn leaves are carotenoids that were present all summer, revealed
when the tree breaks down its chlorophyll to recover the nitrogen. The reds are anthocyanins made
new in the dying leaf, for reasons still disputed, among them a proposed warning signal to aphids.

¶11 W. D. Hamilton & S. P. Brown, “Autumn Tree Colours as a Handicap Signal,” *Proceedings of the
Royal Society of London B* 268 (2001): 1489–93.

¶12 *Shipworm*

¶13 *Teredo navalis* is a clam whose shell has shrunk to a pair of rasping plates. It bores through
submerged wood, eats the shavings, and lines its burrow with calcium carbonate, a method Marc Brunel
is said to have copied in the tunneling shield that dug the Thames Tunnel.

¶14 R. D. Turner, *A Survey and Illustrated Catalogue of the Teredinidae (Mollusca: Bivalvia)*
(Cambridge, Mass.: Museum of Comparative Zoology, Harvard University, 1966).

¶15 II. Water

¶16 *Whalefall*

¶17 A whale carcass on the deep-sea floor supports a succession of communities for decades.
Scavengers strip the flesh, opportunists colonize the enriched sediment, and bacteria breaking down
the oil in the bones release sulfide that feeds chemosynthetic animals, among them the bone-eating
worm *Osedax*.

¶18 C. R. Smith & A. R. Baco, “Ecology of Whale Falls at the Deep-Sea Floor,” in R. N. Gibson & R.
J. A. Atkinson, eds., *Oceanography and Marine Biology: An Annual Review* 41 (2003): 311–54.

¶19 *The False Bottom*

¶20 Wartime sonar found a sound-scattering layer hundreds of meters down that rises toward the
surface at dusk and sinks at dawn. It is made of lanternfish, crustaceans and other animals on the
largest daily migration on earth.

¶21 C. F. Eyring, R. J. Christensen & R. W. Raitt, “Reverberation in the Sea,” *Journal of the
Acoustical Society of America* 20 (1948): 462–75.

¶22 *Why the Sea Is Salt*

¶23 Rain, made slightly acidic by dissolved carbon dioxide, weathers rock. Rivers carry the
dissolved salts to the sea, and evaporation leaves them there. Halley proposed using the sea’s
saltiness as a clock for the age of the earth, but the calculation, attempted by John Joly in 1899,
fails because the ocean removes salt about as fast as rivers add it.

¶24 E. Halley, “A Short Account of the Cause of the Saltness of the Ocean, and of the Several Lakes
That Emit No Rivers; with a Proposal, by Help Thereof, to Discover the Age of the World,”
*Philosophical Transactions* 29, no. 344 (1715): 296.

¶25 *The Frog in the Ice*

¶26 The wood frog survives the freezing of about two-thirds of its body water. Glucose flooded from
its liver keeps the ice outside its cells, and heartbeat, breathing and brain activity stop until
spring.

¶27 K. B. Storey & J. M. Storey, “Biochemical Adaption for Freezing Tolerance in the Wood Frog,
*Rana sylvatica*,” *Journal of Comparative Physiology B* 155 (1984): 29–36.

¶28 III. Air

¶29 *One Twelfth of a Teaspoon*

¶30 A worker honeybee spends her first weeks inside the hive and her last few foraging, and in her
lifetime contributes roughly a twelfth of a teaspoon of honey. The bees fan the nectar until most of
its water is gone, and the resulting low water content and acidity let sealed honey keep almost
indefinitely.

¶31 M. L. Winston, *The Biology of the Honey Bee* (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press,
1987).

¶32 *Globe Skimmer*

¶33 The dragonfly *Pantala flavescens* crosses the Indian Ocean between India and East Africa on the
monsoon winds, breeding in temporary rain pools along the way. The round trip takes several
generations, so no single insect completes it.

¶34 R. C. Anderson, “Do Dragonflies Migrate across the Western Indian Ocean?” *Journal of Tropical
Ecology* 25 (2009): 347–58.

¶35 *Never Landing*

¶36 Miniature data loggers show that common swifts stay airborne almost continuously through their
ten-month non-breeding season, feeding and apparently sleeping on the wing. Young birds may not land
until they first breed.

¶37 A. Hedenström et al., “Annual 10-Month Aerial Life Phase in the Common Swift *Apus apus*,”
*Current Biology* 26 (2016): 3066–70.

¶38 *The Moon Is Leaving*

¶39 Tidal friction transfers the earth’s spin to the moon’s orbit. The day lengthens and the moon
recedes by about 3.8 centimeters a year, as measured by lasers bounced off reflectors the Apollo
crews left behind. Devonian corals record years of about 400 days.

¶40 J. O. Dickey et al., “Lunar Laser Ranging: A Continuing Legacy of the Apollo Program,” *Science*
265 (1994): 482–90.

¶41 IV. Fire

¶42 *Lightning Glass*

¶43 Lightning striking sand fuses fulgurites, branching tubes of glass that trace the discharge.
They contain schreibersite, a phosphide mineral that releases reactive phosphorus in water, so
lightning on the young earth may have supplied much of the phosphorus that prebiotic chemistry
required.

¶44 B. L. Hess, S. Piazolo & J. Harvey, “Lightning Strikes as a Major Facilitator of Prebiotic
Phosphorus Reduction on Early Earth,” *Nature Communications* 12 (2021): 1535.

¶45 *The Missing Fraction*

¶46 In 1972 a small shortfall of uranium-235 in ore from Oklo, Gabon, revealed that natural fission
reactors had run there two billion years ago, with groundwater as the moderator. Xenon isotopes show
that the reaction ran for about thirty minutes, stopped for two and a half hours while the water
boiled off and seeped back, and repeated.

¶47 A. P. Meshik, C. M. Hohenberg & O. V. Pravdivtseva, “Record of Cycling Operation of the Natural
Nuclear Reactor in the Oklo/Okelobondo Area in Gabon,” *Physical Review Letters* 93 (2004): 182302.

¶48 *What the Smoke Wakes*

¶49 Many lodgepole pines bear serotinous cones, sealed with resin that only fire melts, which is why
seedlings carpeted Yellowstone after the fires of 1988. The dormant seeds of many other plants
germinate in response to karrikins, compounds formed in smoke.

¶50 G. R. Flematti et al., “A Compound from Smoke That Promotes Seed Germination,” *Science* 305
(2004): 977.

¶51 *The Light on Your Face*

¶52 Energy released by fusion in the sun’s core works outward by repeated absorption and
re-emission, a random walk estimated to take anywhere from about ten thousand to a few hundred
thousand years. Convection then carries it to the surface, and light crosses to earth in eight
minutes.

¶53 R. Mitalas & K. R. Sills, “On the Photon Diffusion Time Scale for the Sun,” *Astrophysical
Journal* 401 (1992): 759.
